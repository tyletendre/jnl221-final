Border Crossings at Major Ports in the United States

By Ty Letendre

In 2024, legal border crossings reached unusually high levels, and the nation’s busiest ports revealed just how unevenly that traffic is distributed across the United States.

The ports of entry in the United States resemble an interstate highway more than an international boundary. Brake lights are aligned in rows, illuminating commuters inching forward toward inspection booths. Thousands of people cross this boundary every day, whether they are workers heading to their jobs or families visiting relatives. Scenes similar to this unfold daily along the borders of the United States.

In 2024, the level of legal crossings reached alarming levels. According to federal data visualized in this choropleth map, a huge number of crossings occurred nationwide last year, with the overwhelming majority concentrated in just a few states. The map makes a simple but important point: America’s border is not a single place. It is a network of gateways—some under immense pressure, others with far less activity—all tied together by the flow of people and commerce across North America.

This story uses federal data and insight from an expert to help readers understand why border traffic is uneven, how local communities handle the impacts, and why these differences matter for debates on national policy.

Subhead: First Key Finding — Traffic Is Concentrated in a Few States

The choropleth map reveals a pattern in which only a few states dominate border activity. Texas recorded the highest number of entries in 2024, driven by ports such as El Paso and Brownsville. California ranked second, powered by San Ysidro, one of the busiest land crossings in the world. Combined, these states account for most of the legal crossings into the United States.

Many Americans are unaware of how busy the northern border can be. New York and Michigan—shaded darker on the map—processed large numbers of entries in 2024. Buffalo and Detroit account for enormous commercial traffic between U.S. industries and Canadian manufacturers. The map shows a system of borders marked by high-volume gateways alongside states where crossings are far less common.

I spoke with Dr. Elena Varges, an expert on trade policy and migration, who said the map reflects long-standing economic ties rather than a sudden shift in movement. She explained that the activity in places like California and Texas is not new at all—it's the result of deeply embedded economic relationships that have existed for decades.

Subhead: Second Key Finding — Economic Corridors Drive Border Activity

Several factors shaped the pattern seen in 2024. The Southwest hosts major trade corridors that funnel goods between U.S. cities and manufacturing hubs in Mexico. Rail lines, interstate highways, and commercial trucking routes all contribute to the constant traffic at border ports. This geography produces a continuous high volume of legal crossings that anchor economies on both sides of the border.

Commuters also play a major role. In cities like El Paso, thousands of residents cross legally each day for work or school. The northern border tells a similar story. For example, the automotive industry depends on uninterrupted movement between Detroit and Windsor; auto parts may cross multiple times before becoming a finished vehicle.

Meanwhile, states such as North Dakota, Montana, and parts of New England appear lighter on the map. They have fewer major ports, smaller populations, and less commercial traffic, resulting in fewer entries compared with their southern counterparts.

Subhead: What It Means — Uneven Traffic Creates Uneven Challenges

Uneven traffic means uneven burdens. High-volume states must maintain more lanes, more inspectors, more border personnel, and more technology. When infrastructure cannot keep up with demand, wait times grow and supply chains slow. For commuters, that means longer lines and more time lost each day. For commercial carriers, it means delays that ripple through national markets.

These discrepancies also shape federal funding. States with the heaviest demand often require the most investment in inspection stations, security equipment, and road systems. Smaller states rarely receive the same level of support, even though they still manage international movement and infrastructure needs.

The 2024 border-crossing map reveals that the American border functions less like a single waiting line and more like a series of high-pressure gateways that carry immense strain daily. As the United States continues to debate immigration, trade, and economic cooperation, understanding where legal crossings actually occur is critical for supporting both national systems and local communities. For hundreds of thousands of commuters and commercial drivers, the border is not merely a political debate—it is a daily reality.
